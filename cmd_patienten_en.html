Jaw Joint Disorders (CMD)

Jaw clicking, headaches, neck pain — CMD can manifest in many ways. We explain clearly what is behind it and how we can help you.

What does CMD mean?

CMD stands for Craniomandibular Dysfunction — a functional disorder of the jaw joint.

The jaw joint — an extraordinarily active joint

The jaw joint connects the lower jaw to the skull. It is the most heavily used joint in the body — during speaking, chewing and swallowing it moves thousands of times daily. Inside the joint lies a cartilage disc — the so-called articular disc — which acts as a cushion. When the interplay of teeth, muscles and joints is disrupted, CMD develops.

What happens with CMD?

The term CMD encompasses various disorders of the chewing apparatus: functional disorders of the jaw joints, changes to the articular disc, tension in the chewing muscles and misalignment of the teeth. These disorders can occur individually or in combination and reinforce each other.

Muscular CMD
Tension in the chewing muscles
Articular CMD
Joint changes, disc displacement
Kombiniert
Often both forms simultaneously
How common is CMD?

Estimates suggest that 3 to 12% of the population suffer from CMD complaints requiring treatment — women more often than men. Many sufferers do not know that their headaches, tinnitus or neck pain could be related to the jaw. CMD therefore often goes unrecognised for years.

The good news

CMD is very treatable. In most cases no surgical intervention is necessary. Significant and lasting improvements can be achieved with a combined treatment of splint therapy and physiotherapy — provided the causes are identified and treated consistently.

Symptoms — complex and often overlooked

CMD does not always manifest as jaw pain. Many complaints arise far from the jaw.

CMD is often not recognised because the complaints appear at first glance to have nothing to do with the teeth. Neurologists, ENT specialists and orthopaedists often treat only the symptoms — the cause in the jaw remains undiscovered.
Main symptoms — directly at the jaw
Pain in the jaw joint
Dull, persistent or load-dependent pain in one or both jaw joints — at rest or while chewing.
Clicking and grinding sounds
Audible sounds when opening or closing the mouth — often a sign of displacement of the articular disc.
Limited mouth opening
The mouth can no longer be opened fully or without difficulty — sometimes accompanied by a lateral deviation of the lower jaw.
Tense chewing muscles
Hardening and pressure pain in the temporal and chewing muscles, often particularly pronounced in the morning after waking.
Worn teeth
Recognisable tooth wear (grinding facets), loss of incisal edges and sensitive teeth — an indication of nocturnal grinding (bruxism).
Accompanying symptoms — signals from the body

These complaints arise through the close connections between the jaw joint, nerves, muscles and fascia of the entire body.

Tension headaches & migraine
Chronically tense chewing muscles can trigger headaches that extend from the temples to the back of the head. They are often misinterpreted as migraine.
Ear pain & tinnitus
The jaw joint is in the immediate vicinity of the ear. Pain, pressure or ringing in the ear without medical cause can be a CMD signal.
Neck & shoulder pain
Through the close muscular connection between the jaw and neck muscles, CMD tension can radiate far into the back.
Dizziness & visual disturbances
Less common, but possible: balance disorders or visual problems arising from the proximity of the jaw joint to sensitive nerve and muscle structures.
Sleep disorders & fatigue
Nocturnal tooth grinding and the associated muscle stress interrupt sleep — often without those affected noticing.
When to see the dentist? Please visit us if you have several of these complaints simultaneously — especially if they are stronger after waking or increase when chewing. Your first point of contact for jaw joint complaints is always the dentist.
Causes — a multifactorial process

CMD rarely arises from a single cause. Most often several factors work together.

Bruxism — tooth grinding and clenching
The most common cause. Many people unconsciously grind or clench their teeth at night — often amplified by stress. The pressure on teeth, muscles and joints exceeds many times the normal chewing load. Bruxism is simultaneously a cause and symptom of CMD.
Stress & psychological burden
Emotional stress leads to increased muscle tension in the jaw and neck area. Many people involuntarily clench their teeth — not only at night, but also during the day under tension.
Malocclusion & occlusal disorders
A "wrong bite" — i.e. uneven loading when biting together — forces the jaw joint and chewing muscles into a misaligned position. Missing teeth, poorly fitting dentures or orthodontic changes can also promote CMD.
Posture & musculoskeletal factors
The jaw joint and spine are connected through the musculofascial system. A misalignment of the cervical spine can act back on the jaw — and vice versa. Even pelvic or hip misalignments can promote CMD through this chain.
Further triggers
Injuries to the jaw joint (e.g. through accidents), genetic factors, hormonal fluctuations, sleep disorders and underlying conditions such as arthrosis or rheumatic diseases can also contribute to CMD.
Diagnosis — how we proceed
Clinical functional analysis (FAL)

The basis of every CMD diagnosis is the manual functional analysis. We examine by hand, without complex equipment:

1

Medical history & consultation

We take time to understand your medical history: sleep quality, stress, dentures, accidents, previous treatments.

2

Palpation of the chewing muscles

We palpate the chewing muscles, temporal muscles and neck muscles for hardening and sensitive pressure points (trigger points).

3

Examination of the jaw joints

Assessment of joint mobility, measurement of maximum mouth opening and identification of sounds (clicking, grinding).

4

Occlusal analysis

Checking of tooth contacts in the intercuspal position and during movements — premature and interfering contacts can be identified this way.

Extended diagnostics if needed

Depending on findings, we supplement the diagnosis with further procedures: digital X-ray for assessment of the jaw bone, in complex cases an instrumental functional analysis in the articulator (chewing simulator), or — if structural joint changes are suspected — an MRI referral to specialised radiologists.

Splint therapy — the central building block

An individually fabricated occlusal splint is the most frequently used dental treatment for CMD — with good effectiveness and high acceptance.

What does a splint do? It brings the lower jaw into a relaxed, muscle-guided position, relieves the jaw joint, reduces overactivity of the chewing muscles and protects the dental hard tissue from further wear. The precise choice of splint always depends on the individual findings.
Splint types at a glance
Goldstandard
Michigan splint (equilibration splint)
Die am häufigsten eingesetzte und am besten untersuchte Aufbissschiene. Sie wird individuell aus hartem Kunststoff für den Oberkiefer gefertigt und im Artikulator auf die exakten Kieferbewegungen eingestellt. Ziel ist eine harmonische Bisslage mit gleichmäßiger Belastung aller Zähne. Sie wird in der Regel nachts getragen, in manchen Fällen auch tagsüber. Besonders geeignet bei muskulären CMD-Beschwerden und Bruxismus.
Muskelentspannung
Relaxation splint / reflex splint
This splint has a small frontal plateau (anterior plateau) that eliminates lateral tooth contacts. This interrupts parafunctional activities (grinding, clenching) and relieves the chewing muscles. Particularly suitable for muscle tension caused by psycho-emotional burden (stress). It should only be used for short periods.
Gelenkentlastung
Repositioning splint / positioning splint
This splint actively guides the lower jaw into a therapeutic position — usually slightly forward. It is used for disc displacements (displaced articular disc) to relieve structural joint structures and reduce clicking. Its use requires a precise diagnosis, as a long-term change in bite position can occur.
Zahnschutz
Soft splint / night guard
The simplest and most cost-effective option. It consists of soft plastic and protects the teeth from further wear through grinding. However, it has no therapeutic effect on muscle tension or joint position and can even further activate the chewing muscles. It is less suitable for CMD therapy — as pure tooth protection it can be an option.
How long is the splint worn?

Most patients wear the splint every night at first. Initial improvements are often noticeable after just a few weeks. Regular check-up appointments are important: we assess the effect, adjust the splint if necessary and plan further treatment. A CMD splint is not a substitute for therapy — it is a central tool in a comprehensive treatment concept.

Transparency about costs

What does statutory health insurance (GKV) cover?

Die gesetzlichen Krankenkassen übernehmen in der Regel die klinische Funktionsanalyse (FAL) — also die manuelle Examination of the jaw joints und Kaumuskulatur. Die Schienentherapie selbst sowie die instrumentelle Funktionsanalyse sind hingegen in den meisten Fällen Privatleistungen, die von der Kasse nicht oder nur teilweise erstattet werden.

Costs of individually fabricated splints

Costs depend on the type of splint, the manufacturing process and the findings. Simple protective splints from the practice laboratory are cheaper; complex individually customised therapy splints from specialised dental laboratories are more expensive to produce.

Physiotherapy — your partner in CMD treatment

A splint alone is often not enough. Full treatment success is only achieved in combination with physiotherapy.

Manual therapy & jaw mobilisation
The physiotherapist releases tension in the chewing and neck muscles through targeted mobilisation techniques. Restricted jaw mobility can be gradually improved this way.
Trigger point treatment
Hardened muscle points (trigger points) in the jaw, temple and neck area are released through pressure and special techniques — which can relieve headaches and ear pain.
Posture training & body awareness
Many CMD patients display postural abnormalities. The physiotherapist analyses body posture and develops targeted corrections — as the jaw and spine mutually influence each other.
Home exercises
Stretching and relaxation exercises for the chewing muscles, breathing exercises and targeted relaxation techniques that you can apply independently.
Stress management
As stress is considered a key trigger, we additionally recommend relaxation methods such as progressive muscle relaxation, biofeedback or psychological counselling.
Interdisciplinary cooperation

CMD is a complex clinical picture that can rarely be treated by a single specialty alone. Depending on the findings, we work closely with physiotherapists, orthopaedists, ENT specialists, neurologists and, if needed, psychotherapists. The goal: holistic treatment that addresses the roots of your complaints.

Frequently asked questions about CMD treatment
Does the treatment hurt? ▼
The dental examination and splint fitting are completely painless. Physiotherapy can be briefly uncomfortable when treating hardened muscle points — similar to a classic massage. The splint itself generally causes no discomfort after a short settling-in period.
How long does treatment take? ▼
This depends on the severity and cause of the CMD. Initial improvements are often noticeable after just a few weeks of splint therapy. Complete treatment generally takes several months. What is crucial is staying consistent and not stopping treatment prematurely.
Does health insurance pay for CMD treatment? ▼
Statutory health insurance generally covers part of the clinical functional analysis. The individually fabricated CMD splint and the instrumental functional analysis are in many cases private services. We transparently inform you before treatment begins about all costs and prepare a clear treatment plan.
Do I have to wear the splint permanently? ▼
This varies individually. Many patients wear the splint daily at first, later only prophylactically during stress phases. The goal is to achieve lasting relaxation through the combination of splint therapy and physiotherapy, so that the splint is only needed as protection in the long term.
Can CMD be treated without surgery? ▼
In the great majority of cases: yes. Conservative treatments such as splint therapy and physiotherapy lead to significant and lasting improvements. Surgical interventions are rarely necessary and are only considered when all other therapy options have been exhausted.
What can I do myself? ▼
Quite a lot. Pay attention not to unconsciously clench your teeth in everyday life (teeth should lightly touch at rest but not press). Regular relaxation exercises, sufficient sleep and stress reduction help noticeably. The exercises recommended by your physiotherapist should be carried out consistently. And: wear your splint regularly.
